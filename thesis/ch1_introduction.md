# Chapter 1. Introduction
## 1.1 Motivation

In Francophone West Africa, the baccalauréat is the single high-stakes examination that gates access to higher education, and success rates are low. In Côte d'Ivoire specifically, the national BAC pass rate was 40.15% in 2025, with only 133,518 of 332,515 candidates admitted [APAnews, 2025]. It has stayed in that band across the past decade (roughly 29–46%; e.g. 32.09% in 2023) [Yeclo, 2023]. Within the baccalauréat, the série C track (the mathematics- and physical-science-intensive stream) is both the most selective and the most consequential for entry into engineering, medicine, and the quantitative disciplines.

Performance in Terminale C mathematics is therefore a meaningful determinant of a student's trajectory, but the resources a student can bring to bear on it are unevenly distributed. Qualified mathematics teachers are scarce (a shortage acute enough that a March 2026 exceptional state recruitment drew 7,668 candidates for 2,000 contractual mathematics and physical-science posts [Connectionivoirienne, 2026]), and access to them is better in urban centres than rural areas, while structured revision outside the classroom is hard to come by. The financially fortunate hire private tutors, but at a price out of reach for the majority: a well-known Abidjan tutor put competent tutoring at on the order of €100 per month [Le savant ACHI, personal interview, 2 February 2026], close to the Ivorian minimum monthly wage (the SMIG, about 75,000 FCFA ≈ €114 [Gouv.CI, 2023]). An Ivorian family thus spends nearly a full minimum wage on tutoring, where in France the same minimum wage (the SMIC, ≈ €1,800/month [Travail-Emploi.gouv.fr, 2026]) makes the relative burden far lighter. Good private tutoring is concentrated among families who can afford it and in the cities where tutors are present. This access asymmetry is the practical problem that motivates this thesis, whose aim is to give students affordable tools for self-study.

Large language models (LLMs) appear, at first sight, to offer a route to closing that gap: a conversational tutor available at any hour to any student with a connected device. But the naïve application of a general-purpose LLM to high-stakes mathematics tutoring is problematic in three specific ways that this thesis takes as central: two concerning what and how the model answers, and a third, compounding risk in the reliability of those answers.

First, a general-purpose model is not aligned to a specific curriculum. It may solve a problem by a method the student has not been taught, invoke a theorem outside the syllabus, or use notation and conventions that diverge from the official West African programme. For a Terminale C student preparing for a specific examination against a specific textbook, this curriculum drift is itself a failure mode, independent of mathematical correctness: a technically valid solution that departs from the prescribed method is, for this student, a wrong answer. This is the gap that grounding is meant to close, and it is the principal motivation for the retrieval-based approach taken here.

Second, access to the most capable models is, in practice, rationed. The frontier models a student can reach for free impose daily usage limits that a serious revision session exhausts quickly; the models without such limits cost money the student does not have. The practical question this raises is whether a small, locally deployable model, cheap enough to run without per-query cost or hard usage caps, can be made good enough to serve as that tutor once it is properly grounded. This is the deployment question behind the generator comparison of H4.

A general-purpose model carries a further, well-documented risk that compounds the first: LLMs hallucinate: they produce fluent, confident statements that are factually or mathematically false [Huang et al., 2025]. This is a general property of how these models generate text, not a phenomenon specific to any one region or curriculum, and this thesis makes no claim about its measured prevalence among Ivorian students in particular. Its danger here is one of amplification: hallucination is most harmful precisely where the learner cannot readily verify the output, and a Terminale C student facing an unfamiliar derivation is, by definition, poorly placed to tell a correct one from a plausible-looking wrong one. In that setting, fluent error is more dangerous than visible failure, because it is more likely to be believed and carried forward. The thesis therefore treats faithfulness (the model's adherence to its grounded sources rather than to its own unconstrained generation).

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [Lewis et al., 2020] is the natural first step against the first and third of these dangers. By grounding the model's output in retrieved passages from an authoritative curriculum source, RAG constrains the tutor to the syllabus and gives it a factual basis against which faithfulness can be enforced and audited. RAG is, moreover, an established hallucination-mitigation approach in the broader literature [Lewis et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2025]. The authoritative source in the Ivorian context is the CIAM series (Collection Inter-Africaine de Mathématiques), the canonical regional textbook aligned with the official Terminale C programme. This thesis builds and evaluates a RAG-based mathematics tutor grounded in the CIAM Terminale C corpus and, addressing the second danger, pairs that pipeline with a small language model (SLM) whose grounded response quality is compared against that of a frontier LLM of the kind students currently use, to test whether a low-cost, locally deployable tutor can approach frontier quality once both are grounded.

Beyond the immediate artefact, the work is intended to be decision-relevant for the people choosing whether and how to fund AI for education in the region. For entrepreneurs and government bodies weighing investment in AI-based learning tools (a space drawing growing public and private investment in the region, with national education budgets in the billions of euros and a nascent EdTech startup ecosystem [TeamFrance-Export, 2025], and with Côte d'Ivoire among the lead countries developing a national AI-in-education competency framework under the GPE-KIX/UNESCO initiative [GPE-KIX, 2025]), a grounded, curriculum-aligned evaluation provides evidence on whether such tools can deliver curriculum-faithful tutoring at acceptable quality, and at what cost, rather than leaving the question to intuition.

The francophone, curriculum-specific, low-resource character of this setting is not incidental. Most published work on LLM tutors and on retrieval for education targets English and draws on English-language resources; the EdTech tools that reach West African students are predominantly anglophone and curriculum-agnostic. Building and, critically, evaluating a tutor for a francophone curriculum with scarce labelled is very rare and addressing this is part of the contribution of this work.

## 1.2 Problem statement

This thesis is organised around three gaps between what general-purpose RAG offers and what a mathematics-focused francophone West African curriculum tutor requires. A fourth, lighter question (whether a grounded small model can stand in for a frontier model at acceptable quality and cost) is opened rather than closed by this work, and is treated as the deployment question behind H4 (§1.3) rather than as a fully resolved gap.

Gap 1: generic retrieval is content-type-blind. Standard retrieval, whether sparse, dense, or a hybrid of the two, ranks candidate passages by a single relevance signal and is indifferent to what kind of content it returns. But solving a Terminale C problem requires the student to mobilise both the underlying theory (a definition, theorem, or method) and a worked example demonstrating its application; the pedagogical value of pairing the two is well established [Sweller, 1988]. A content-type-blind retriever can return five theory passages and no worked example, or vice versa, and either failure degrades the tutor's pedagogical utility. Closing this gap requires retrieval that is aware of, and enforces, a pedagogically motivated content-type mix.

Gap 2: LLM-as-judge evaluation is rarely calibrated against domain experts. Evaluating a tutor's free-text responses at scale is infeasible by human annotation alone, and the field has increasingly turned to using a strong LLM as an automated judge [Zheng et al., 2023]. But an uncalibrated LLM judge risks two distinct failures in this education setting. It may simply be unreliable, disagreeing with expert human judgement to a degree that makes its scores uninformative. And it may be circular: if the same family of model both generates and judges, and if "quality" is operationalised as "what the judge model finds good," then the evaluation rewards the generator for sharing the judge's biases rather than for tutoring well, and any comparison of generators against that judge is compromised. Closing this gap requires a judge whose agreement with domain-expert human judgement is demonstrated rather than assumed, and an evaluation framework whose notion of quality is anchored to that human judgement rather than to model preference.

Gap 3: the francophone, low-resource, curriculum-specific setting is under-studied. The methods above (hybrid retrieval, pedagogical retrieval, calibrated LLM-judging) have to be made to work in French, over OCR'd textbook content dense with mathematical notation, with a labelling budget of a small curriculum-proficient panel rather than a crowd. Each of these conditions interacts with the methods in ways that the anglophone, more data-rich literature does not address, and documenting those interactions, including where they constrain what can be concluded, is part of the problem this thesis takes on.

## 1.3 Research questions and hypotheses

The umbrella research question of this thesis is:

> What form of retrieval-augmented generation can be adapted to the pedagogical structure of a high-stakes mathematics curriculum, and how can the resulting tutor be evaluated rigorously when domain-expert annotation is scarce and the targeted population is low-resource?

This decomposes into four sub-questions, each operationalised as a testable hypothesis. The hypotheses are stated here and developed in full in the methodology of Chapter 3.

- RQ1 / H1 (retrieval channel). Does fusing sparse (BM25) and dense (multilingual) retrieval improve retrieval quality over dense-only retrieval for francophone curriculum mathematics? H1: a reciprocal-rank-fusion hybrid outperforms dense-only retrieval on standard ranking metrics against the human-panel relevance judgements (Chapter 3).

- RQ2 / H2 (pedagogical retrieval, a central contribution). Does enforcing a pedagogically motivated content-type mix (theory passages together with worked examples) at retrieval time improve tutoring quality beyond content-type-blind hybrid retrieval? H2: a pedagogical retriever that selects a fixed mix of theory-type and example-type passages from the hybrid candidate pool outperforms the content-type-blind hybrid baseline.

- RQ3 / H3 (evaluation calibration). When expert labels are scarce, can an LLM be calibrated to act as a relevance assessor, reproducing the human panel's (query, chunk) relevance judgements faithfully enough to substitute for them at scale? H3: an LLM relevance assessor, prompted with the same rubric the panel used and applied to the (query, chunk) pairs, (i) produces qrels that rank the four retrievers (dense, BM25, hybrid, pedagogical) in the same order as the human-panel qrels, and (ii) reaches per-item agreement with held-out annotators (Krippendorff's α) that approaches the human inter-annotator agreement ceiling, rather than meeting a fixed numerical threshold. The ceiling is that of a mixed-expertise panel (two teachers and eight Terminale C students; Chapter 3), and is read in that light.

- RQ4 / H4 (deployment feasibility). When properly grounded, do open-weight small language models close the quality gap with frontier models, to a degree that would make low-cost local deployment feasible? H4: under grounding, the frontier–open quality gap (measured as a difference-in-differences against the ungrounded gap) narrows substantially.

The status of each hypothesis differs, and this thesis is explicit about that difference throughout (§1.4, and the BUILT/PLANNED convention of Chapter 3). H1 and H2 (retrieval side) are tested end-to-end: the (query, chunk) relevance-annotation package was dispatched, returned, and scored, and the populated results are reported in Chapter 4. H3's human inter-annotator ceiling is computed from the overlap-annotated anchor, and the reframed H3 relevance assessor that it validates was run on those same returned labels and is likewise reported. The original formulation of H3 (an LLM grading full tutoring responses on a four-criterion rubric) is reframed to future work (and delegated externally as a no-cost freeroll), because it rests on a separate response-grading annotation campaign which responses took more time to collect before submission of this report. H4 is fully specified but not entirely executed (the human calibration is pending).

## 1.4 Contributions

This thesis makes four contributions. They are stated here with explicit attention to what has been executed versus what has been specified but not executed at the time of writing, so that the scope of each claim is unambiguous from the outset.

C1 — Pedagogical retrieval (novel method; H2). The principal methodological contribution is pedagogical retrieval: a retrieval-time selection policy that enforces a pedagogically motivated mix of theory-type and worked-example-type passages, drawn from the same fused candidate pool as a standard hybrid retriever and differing from it only in selection. Its retrieval-side evaluation against the human-panel relevance judgements was executed end-to-end, and the populated results are reported in Chapter 4 (§4.3). Its generation-side evaluation (via the response judge of C2 and the student study of Chapter 3) is specified but not yet executed.

C2 — A human-calibrated, model-independent evaluation framework, and a validated LLM relevance assessor (H3). The second contribution is an evaluation framework built around a relevance rubric whose notion of quality is anchored to a correct human solution and is deliberately model-independent, avoiding the circularity that would arise from defining relevance as "what an LLM finds useful." The framework comprises the rubric, the annotation protocol with its overlap design, and the agreement machinery (Krippendorff's α, qrels construction, ranking metrics); the rubric design, the annotation tooling, and a two-teacher calibration pilot are executed, the pilot having driven a documented revision of the rubric (v0.1 → v1.0), and the full dispatch, to a mixed panel of two teachers and eight Terminale C students, has been sent and returned. On this foundation, H3 asks whether an LLM relevance assessor (the same model, prompted with the panel's rubric) can reproduce the panel's (query, chunk) judgements well enough to stand in for them: validated primarily by whether its qrels rank the retrieval systems as the human-panel qrels do, and secondarily by per-item agreement approaching the human inter-annotator ceiling rather than a fixed α bar. This adopts an established, independently reproduced IR-evaluation method [Faggioli et al., 2023; Thomas et al., 2024; Upadhyay et al., 2024] and contributes its domain-transfer validation to a francophone, low-resource, curriculum-mathematics setting, calibrated against a curriculum-proficient human panel rather than crowd workers. 

C3 — A documented, reproducible Ivorian Terminale C corpus and data card (artifact). The third contribution is a concrete, reusable research artifact: a chunked, content-type-tagged corpus of 722 chunks drawn from ten CIAM Terminale C chapters and two BAC série C papers, with a full data card reporting corpus statistics, OCR-quality validation, a documented eight-value content-type taxonomy, and an explicit account of the corpus's limitations (figure dropping, geometry exclusion, a quantified worked-example tagging hole, and leakage discipline via held-out BAC 2025). To the author's knowledge this is the first openly documented RAG corpus for the Ivorian Terminale C mathematics curriculum. 

C4 — A specification of a grounded frontier-versus-open generator comparison (H4). The fourth contribution is a fully specified experimental design for testing whether grounding closes the quality gap between open-weight small models and frontier models, framed as a difference-in-differences against the ungrounded gap, with the metric, the grounding manipulation, and the analysis pre-specified. 


## 1.5 Scope and non-claims

The boundaries of this work are stated up front.

- Geometry is out of scope. The corpus comprises ten non-geometry chapters of the CIAM Terminale C textbook; geometry chapters are excluded because their constitutive dependence on figures is not fully preserved through the OCR pipeline (Chapter 3, §M3.4). All claims, including H1 and H2, are claims about Terminale C content excluding geometry.
- A single official textbook. All curricular content is drawn from the official CIAM textbook series. Third-party textbooks that many students use for revision outside class are not represented, and generalisation to them is not directly supported by the experiments here.
- The tutor is single-turn. The generation pipeline answers a single question against retrieved context; multi-turn dialogue is not implemented and is identified as future work.

Stating these limits at the outset is deliberate. A central aim of the thesis is to demonstrate methodological discipline under real constraints (scarce expert labels, OCR'd low-resource content, a hard deadline), and an honest account of what the work does not establish is part of that demonstration.
